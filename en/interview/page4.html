nobunoza　Chuo, Tokyo
Renovation completed 2015 (visited April 2016)

A new kind of shop, half bar and half retail, born of a Timeless Living renovation

nobunoza, a shop stocked only with fine things — handmade leather bags, wallets and umbrellas commissioned in Italy — and Bar Ginza nozaki, a quiet retreat for grown-ups known to those who know. Mr N has run both in Ginza, and has worked with Timeless Living for some nine years; each shop has been renovated by us once before. This time his brief was a novel one: put the bar and the retail together. The renovation produced a shop of a new kind, with striking blue walls.

— How did you come to ask Timeless Living for a third renovation?
Friends in Central Asia and in Scandinavia had been telling me for years that they live well, and I had long wanted to go and see that life properly for myself. But doing so would mean closing up for a long stretch, and paying rent on two shops the whole time would be a waste. So I decided to bring them together into one, and to do the thing I had had in mind for a while: sell goods and serve food and drink in the same place. If it was to be one shop it would be Bar Ginza nozaki — and in March 2015 I talked it over with my friend Mr H at Timeless Living.
— What exactly did you ask for?
I said I wanted to attach nobunoza's retail to half of Bar Ginza nozaki. Mr H's first words were, hmm, what size was that again. But he had often said that the bar ought to carry nobunoza's goods anyway, so he had a picture of it almost at once. I have always believed it is best to leave things to a professional you trust, and that an amateur putting his oar in produces nothing good. I had been happy with the two previous renovations, so this time too I left it, essentially, to Timeless Living.
— The blue walls are striking in the new interior.
The blue walls and the inside faces of the doors are the defining thing. It came from Mr H: the retail area would trace the shape of Italy in plan — the boot — and the walls would stand for the Mediterranean sky and sea, painted a blue that suits Mr N. So the existing Hip lime plaster walls were painted blue over the top. Painting plaster with an ordinary paint coats the surface like nail varnish, but with paint from the English maker Farrow & Ball, apparently, almost nothing of the character is lost. He recommended Skylight, a calm blue, and showed me samples taped to the wall; I could not really tell, so I left it to him. It is remarkable that a professional can picture the finished thing from a small sample.
When the walls were white I hung two photographs. Skylight sets paintings off nicely, so now there are six, including oils by a painter from Florence. I also like the way the colour shifts a good deal with daylight and with the lighting. We change the output of the halogens three times a day; turn them down and the walls read as a warm, greenish colour.
— The glazing frame at the entrance was to have been black at first, I gather.
The idea was a glass partition with black frames and a solid door, as at the old nobunoza, so that long-standing customers would find a familiar scene. But partway through the work Mr H said that for a bar, black frames give too little sense of enclosure and would leave people unsettled. In the end we agreed: keep the door design, and take the frames in Skylight, the same colour as the walls. I think it was the right call.
— What else stands out?
We are on the top floor, and — unusually for Ginza — there is a dome in the ceiling with a rooflight, so making use of daylight is a large part of it. As a bar we opened only in the evening and saw no need for the rooflight, so it had been boarded over. The combined shop opens from midday, and the explanation was that daylight through the rooflight would strengthen the Mediterranean idea during the day. He also said that as the wall colour turned through different blues with the daylight, the room would register the season, the weather and the hour.
The ceiling and dome had been a greyish white; this time they were changed to a bright, cream-leaning white so that the daylight would count for more. The timber trim around the dome was taken in the same colour so that the two read as one. The result opens straight up, the height tells, and the ceiling has become something like a sky. Long-standing customers are surprised: so there was a rooflight.
An oval table sits directly beneath the rooflight, so that goods can be looked at in daylight and people can eat and drink with the night sky above them. According to Mr H it is Sardinia, afloat in the Mediterranean (laughs). An oval is easy to move around, and easy to seat more people at.
And on the bookshelf Mr H proposed — indispensable, he said, in a room imagined as a man living alone — I keep the books I have enjoyed lately.
— How have customers responded?
Very well, I am glad to say. People tell me it feels as settled as a shop that has been here for years. This was true before too: the bar side is an open kitchen, so I can talk to customers across the counter, and that counts for a lot. The counter is at the height of an ordinary dining table, low for a bar, and the glass-fronted cupboard I have always used deliberately shows the glasses — that may be part of why people find it comfortable. I have come to see that tableware matters as part of the interior. Mr H told me that rather than a simple interior made by subtraction, we would build it up by addition, the way interiors abroad are made; and I can feel how comfortable addition is.
We open from noon until midnight, so some people come for a drink and buy something while they are here, and others come to buy and stay for a glass. At the end of last year someone came in at about ten in the evening, on the way home from work. A drink, a bit of conversation this way and that, and an hour taken over a purchase. It is a real pleasure to hear people say they enjoy spending time like that.
“It was a measure born of necessity, but having done it, it has turned out very well. You could build an ordinary living room like this, I think,” says Mr N, visibly enjoying himself. Good drink and good things to eat with it (including an excellent curry in which the beef falls apart), fine leather goods, and conversation that is both clever and good fun: the new nobunoza takes what was appealing about two shops and makes more of it — a place for grown-ups to play.
